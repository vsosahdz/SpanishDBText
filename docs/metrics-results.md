# Metrics Results Analysis

*[Leer en español](es/metrics-results.md)*

This document presents a critical analysis of the metrics computed for the three bilingual datasets generated by the translation pipeline: WikiText (1,000 articles), FEVER (9,998 articles), and Europarl (1,005 debates). The goal is to assess the real quality of the generated datasets and flag any anomalies.


*Updated 2026-10-11: the numbers below were recomputed on the released corpus, after five WikiText entries were translated again and FEVER entry 4148 was repaired; see [../corpus/README.md](../corpus/README.md#caveats).*

## 1. General Corpus Metrics

### Summary Table

| Sents/Doc (mean) | 129.7 | 127.6 | 8.2 | 8.0 | 276.5 | 278.2 |

### Observations

**Token and type counts are consistent.** Spanish consistently has more tokens (6-11% more) and more types (17-57% more) than English across all three corpora. The higher type count in Spanish is expected due to morphological richness (verb conjugations, gendered adjectives, inflected forms). The token expansion is within the expected 5-15% range for EN-to-ES translation.

**Europarl has the smallest vocabulary despite the largest token count.** With 6.95M tokens but only 36,790 English types (vs WikiText's 84,029 types on 2.85M tokens), this is not anomalous — it reflects the repetitive, formulaic nature of parliamentary language. Debates reuse the same political and procedural vocabulary extensively. This is confirmed by the lower hapax percentage (28.6% vs WikiText's 39.7%).

### Anomaly: WikiText minimum word count

The WikiText corpus was filtered to include only articles with 1,000+ words, but the metrics show a minimum of 807 English words. Investigation reveals 49 documents below the 1,000-word threshold. This discrepancy is **not a data error** — it is caused by a difference in tokenization. The corpus selection stage used Python's `str.split()` for word counting (which counts hyphenated words and contractions as single tokens), while the metrics use a regex-based tokenizer (`\b\w+\b`) that splits on non-alphanumeric characters, producing fewer tokens per document. The difference is small (3-20% in edge cases) and does not affect the quality of the corpus.

### Anomaly: FEVER minimum word count

FEVER shows a minimum of 76 English words despite a 100-word filter. The same tokenization discrepancy applies: the filter used one counting method, the metrics use another. Additionally, FEVER's Spanish side shows a minimum of 71 words — below even the English minimum. This means at least one article's translation is shorter than the original, which is unusual for EN-to-ES translation. These entries may warrant manual inspection.

### Summary Table

| Hapax % | 39.72% | 40.01% | 44.31% | 43.91% | 28.63% | 31.74% |
| Dis Legomena % | 14.08% | 14.46% | 14.52% | 14.75% | 11.22% | 12.30% |
| Top-100 Coverage | 42.17% | 45.28% | 46.84% | 50.03% | 53.77% | 52.53% |
| Top-1000 Coverage | 64.66% | 63.82% | 73.51% | 73.29% | 80.90% | 77.72% |

### Observations

**All Zipf exponents are in the natural language range (0.89-0.99).** This is a strong positive signal — it confirms that the LLM-generated translations maintain natural word frequency distributions. If the translations were mechanical or formulaic, we would see deviations from the expected ~1.0 exponent. The fact that all six measurements (3 corpora x 2 languages) fall within the expected range validates the naturalness of the translated text.

**Europarl is the most Zipfian (0.985 EN).** Parliamentary text is highly structured and formulaic, which produces a frequency distribution very close to the theoretical Zipf law. This makes sense — speakers reuse standard political vocabulary and procedural phrases.

**WikiText has the lowest Zipf exponent (0.897 EN).** This suggests slightly more vocabulary diversity than expected — the "long tail" of rare words is more prominent in encyclopedic text. This is consistent with WikiText covering many different topics with specialized terminology.

**Hapax legomena percentages are healthy.** WikiText and FEVER show ~40-44% hapax, which is typical for natural language corpora. Europarl's lower 28-31% reflects its repetitive vocabulary. The EN and ES percentages are remarkably similar within each corpus, indicating that the translations preserve the diversity structure of the originals.

**Vocabulary coverage shows expected patterns.** Europarl's top 100 words cover 53% of all tokens (highly concentrated), while WikiText's top 100 cover only 42% (more diverse). FEVER falls in between. These patterns are consistent with the domain characteristics of each corpus.

## 3. Lexical Diversity Metrics

### Summary Table

| Metric | WikiText EN | WikiText ES | FEVER EN | FEVER ES | Europarl EN | Europarl ES |
|--------|-------------|-------------|----------|----------|-------------|-------------|
| Corpus TTR | 0.0294 | 0.0344 | 0.0308 | 0.0326 | 0.0053 | 0.0083 |
| RTTR | 49.72 | 60.04 | 40.82 | 45.40 | 13.95 | 21.88 |
| MATTR (w=100) | 0.7081 | 0.6948 | 0.6903 | 0.6701 | 0.7146 | 0.7170 |
| MTLD | 84.63 | 77.27 | 72.86 | 63.24 | 89.74 | 91.47 |
| Per-doc TTR (mean) | 0.335 | 0.335 | 0.606 | 0.577 | 0.275 | 0.301 |
| Per-doc MATTR (mean) | 0.707 | 0.694 | 0.677 | 0.663 | 0.712 | 0.716 |
| Per-doc MTLD (mean) | 84.69 | 77.83 | 68.44 | 61.19 | 89.29 | 91.54 |

### Observations

**MATTR values are consistent and healthy (0.67-0.72).** All six measurements fall in the 0.67-0.72 range, which is characteristic of well-written, natural text. Values below 0.60 would suggest repetitive or formulaic language. Values above 0.80 would suggest artificially diverse text. The tight range across corpora and languages is a positive quality indicator.

**Europarl has the highest MTLD (89-91) despite the lowest vocabulary.** This seems counterintuitive but makes sense: MTLD measures how many consecutive words you can read before diversity drops — in parliamentary text, speakers construct long, varied sentences even though they draw from a limited political vocabulary. The text flows well without excessive repetition within sentences.

**FEVER has the lowest MTLD (63-72).** Short factual passages have less room for lexical variation. With a median document length of only 139 words, FEVER entries simply do not have enough text to sustain high diversity over long stretches.

**Spanish MTLD is consistently lower than English for WikiText and FEVER, but higher for Europarl.** The WikiText and FEVER pattern (ES MTLD < EN MTLD) is expected because Spanish uses more function words (articles, prepositions) that repeat frequently. The Europarl reversal is interesting — it may be because the Spanish translations of parliamentary text use a richer set of verb forms and synonyms than the English originals.

**Per-document TTR shows the expected length effect.** FEVER documents (short) have per-doc TTR of 0.60, while Europarl documents (long) have only 0.28. This confirms why TTR alone is unreliable — it is dominated by document length. MATTR and MTLD, being length-independent, provide a much more meaningful comparison.

## 4. Syntactic Metrics

### Summary Table

| Metric | WikiText EN | WikiText ES | FEVER EN | FEVER ES | Europarl EN | Europarl ES |
|--------|-------------|-------------|----------|----------|-------------|-------------|
| Sent Length (mean) | 22.0 | 23.8 | 21.4 | 24.4 | 25.0 | 24.9 |
| Sent Length (median) | 21 | 22 | 20 | 22 | 22 | 22 |
| Sent Length (std) | 11.9 | 12.9 | 12.8 | 14.4 | 15.2 | 15.2 |
| Sent Length (max) | 403 | 501 | 469 | 658 | 1,584 | 1,616 |

### Observations

**Spanish sentences are consistently longer than English.** Across all three corpora, Spanish averages 1-3 more words per sentence than English. This is expected — Spanish requires more function words (articles, prepositions) and has longer verb phrases than English.

**Europarl has the highest mean sentence length (25 words).** Parliamentary speech tends toward longer, more complex sentence structures with subordinate clauses, which is reflected in the data.

### Anomaly: Extreme maximum sentence lengths

All three corpora show suspiciously long maximum sentence lengths: WikiText 403/501 words, FEVER 469/658 words, Europarl 1,584/1,616 words. Sentences of 400+ words are almost certainly not real sentences — they are artifacts of the sentence splitter failing on text that lacks proper punctuation. This is a **limitation of the simple regex-based sentence splitter** (`[.!?]` boundaries), not a data quality issue. Possible causes:

- Long lists or tables without sentence-ending punctuation
- Europarl speaker blocks that span multiple paragraphs without periods
- FEVER entries with semicolons or colons used instead of periods

This does not affect the quality of the translations, but it means that sentence-level statistics (especially the max and std) should be interpreted with caution. The mean and median values are reliable.

### Anomaly: Zero-length sentences

WikiText EN has 9 zero-length sentences, and FEVER EN has 44. These are artifacts of the sentence splitter producing empty strings when splitting on consecutive punctuation marks (e.g., `"..."` or `"?!"` patterns). Again, this is a splitter limitation, not a data issue.

## 5. Quality & Semantic Metrics

### Summary Table

| Metric | WikiText | FEVER | Europarl |

| Expansion ratio (mean) | 1.044 | 1.091 | 1.051 |
| Expansion ratio (std) | 0.036 | 0.066 | 0.021 |

| Exact duplicates EN | 0 | 11 | 0 |
| Exact duplicates ES | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| Near-duplicates EN | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| Near-duplicates ES | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| Stopwords EN % | 36.67% | 33.16% | 43.16% |
| Stopwords ES % | 38.71% | 38.89% | 40.68% |
| Special chars EN % | 3.08% | 3.24% | 3.23% |
| Special chars ES % | 2.47% | 2.44% | 3.05% |
| Uppercase EN % | 3.56% | 5.66% | 4.51% |
| Uppercase ES % | 3.15% | 4.15% | 3.98% |

### Observations

**Expansion ratios are within normal range (1.04-1.09).** The expansion ratio is computed as `len(spanish_text) / len(english_text)` in characters. English-to-Spanish translation typically produces 10-20% more text. Our ratios of 4-9% expansion are slightly lower than the typical range, but this is reasonable because proper nouns (which are common in all three corpora) do not expand. The low standard deviation in Europarl (0.021) indicates very consistent translation behavior for parliamentary text.

**Europarl has the tightest expansion distribution.** With min=0.940 and max=1.171, no Europarl document has a problematic expansion ratio. This suggests the chunking and review stages worked especially well for structured parliamentary text.

### Anomaly: FEVER has 11 exact duplicate English texts

Investigation reveals these duplicates come from two clusters of articles with nearly identical content in the original FEVER corpus:

- **Cluster 1** (6 articles): Articles about "College Baseball All-America Team" and "All-American Team" from different years. These share identical Wikipedia passages because the same template text was used for multiple years.
- **Cluster 2** (5 articles): Articles about "Super Bowl of Poker" from years 1979-1989. Same issue — identical template passages reused across years.

These duplicates exist in the **original FEVER corpus** and passed through the selection filter because they have different FEVER IDs (different article titles). The Spanish translations are all unique (0 duplicates in ES), which means the pipeline translated each one independently and produced slightly different Spanish text each time. This is actually a good sign for translation diversity, but the source duplicates should be noted as a dataset limitation. They represent 0.11% of the FEVER corpus (11 out of 9,998).

### Anomaly: FEVER has 31 articles with expansion ratio below 0.85

31 FEVER articles have Spanish translations that are significantly shorter than the English original (ratio < 0.85). The worst case has a ratio of 0.568 — meaning the translation is only 56.8% the length of the original. These are likely cases where:

- The translation was truncated (despite the chunking and truncation detection)
- The model summarized instead of translating
- The programmatic truncation check did not catch the issue (since it only overrides the evaluation score, it does not re-translate)

**These 31 articles (0.31% of FEVER) should be considered low-quality entries.** For a corpus of 9,998 entries, this is an acceptable error rate, but users of the corpus should be aware of it.

Additionally, WikiText has 91 articles where the Spanish translation is shorter than the English original (expansion ratio < 1.0, measured in characters). Investigation of these articles reveals three distinct categories:

**3 articles with ratio < 0.90 (real truncation).** The worst case is article 766 ("Good Shepherd (song)", ratio 0.794), where the Spanish translation ends mid-sentence ("contó con una rara voz principal...") and is missing the final 8 paragraphs of the original. Notably, the individual Stage 3 candidates had normal ratios (1.045 and 1.049), which means the truncation occurred during the Stage 4 consensus merge — the reviewer model failed to include all sections when combining candidates. The pipeline's evaluator assigned a completeness score of 70 to this article, correctly detecting the problem, but no re-translation was triggered since the evaluation stage only scores, it does not re-process.

**8 articles with ratio 0.90-0.95 (possible partial truncation).** These are borderline cases where some content may have been lost during the merge stage. They should be reviewed manually.

**80 articles with ratio 0.95-1.0 (legitimate compression).** Analysis shows a clear pattern: 52% of all sub-1.0 articles are classified as "entertainment" or "music" (vs 26% in the full corpus), and 38% contain sections with "credits", "charts", or "certifications" — structured data sections full of proper nouns, numbers, and abbreviations that do not expand when translated. This is normal behavior, not a quality issue. For example, a discography section listing track names, record labels, and chart positions will have the same length in both languages.

In summary, only 3 out of 1,000 WikiText articles (0.3%) have a genuine truncation problem. The remaining 88 sub-1.0 articles are explained by legitimate content characteristics.

### Anomaly: FEVER has 2 articles with expansion ratio above 1.5

Two FEVER articles have unusually high expansion ratios (1.528 and 1.646). These are:
- Article 4523: "14 inch gun" (487 EN chars, 744 ES chars)
- Article 4563: "1949-51 Soviet nuclear tests" (987 EN chars, 1,625 ES chars)

These may be cases where the translator added explanatory text or where abbreviations in English were expanded to full phrases in Spanish (e.g., "14 in." becoming "cañón de 14 pulgadas"). While not errors per se, they are outliers worth noting.

**Stopword percentages are natural.** English ranges from 33-43% and Spanish from 38-41%. Europarl has the highest English stopword rate (43%) because parliamentary speech uses more function words. The Spanish percentages are more consistent across corpora, which is typical — Spanish has a more stable function word distribution regardless of domain.

**Special character and uppercase percentages are normal.** All values are in the 2-6% range with no anomalies. Spanish consistently has slightly lower special character and uppercase percentages than English, which is expected (Spanish capitalizes fewer words and uses fewer abbreviations).

### Classification distribution

- **WikiText** shows reasonable topical diversity with 18+ categories, dominated by "history" (284) and "entertainment" (260). However, there is an inconsistency: both "Science" and "science" appear as separate categories (6 and 101 entries), indicating a case-sensitivity issue in the metadata extraction.
- **FEVER** is dominated by "sports" (6,675 out of 9,998 = 66.8%), as expected and documented.
- **Europarl** is dominated by "politics" (483 out of 1,005 = 48.1%), which makes sense for parliamentary proceedings.

### Anomaly: WikiText classification case inconsistency

The WikiText classification distribution shows both "Science" (capitalized, 6 entries) and "science" (lowercase, 101 entries) as separate categories. This is a bug in the metadata extraction stage — the LLM sometimes returns the classification with different capitalization. This affects only the metadata, not the translation quality. The fix would be to normalize classification strings to lowercase in the pipeline.

## 6. Cross-Dataset Quality Assessment

### Overall Quality Grades

| Dataset | Quality Assessment | Key Strengths | Key Concerns |
|---------|-------------------|---------------|--------------|
| **Europarl** | Excellent | Tightest expansion ratios (std=0.021), 0 duplicates, highest lexical diversity (MTLD=91), most consistent translations | None significant |
| **WikiText** | Good | 0 duplicates, natural Zipf distribution, good lexical diversity | 3 articles (0.3%) with real truncation; 80 articles with legitimate compression from structured content; classification case inconsistency |
| **FEVER** | Good with caveats | Largest corpus (9,998), natural frequency distributions, diverse topics | 11 exact duplicates from source; 31 articles with expansion < 0.85; strong sports bias (66.8%) |

### Positive Findings

1. **All Zipf exponents are in the natural language range (0.89-0.99).** The LLM-generated translations behave like natural text, not machine-generated formulaic output.

2. **Zero near-duplicates in sampled comparisons.** The near-duplicate detection (Jaccard similarity on 5-word shingles, threshold 0.85) found no pairs in any corpus. However, this result should be interpreted with caution: due to computational cost, near-duplicate detection uses a random sample of 500 documents per corpus, covering only ~0.25% of all possible pairs in FEVER (9,998 documents). While the absence of near-duplicates in the sample is encouraging, it does not guarantee zero near-duplicates in the full corpus. A more exhaustive detection method (e.g., MinHash with LSH) would be needed for definitive results.

3. **MATTR values are consistent (0.67-0.72).** Lexical diversity is stable and healthy across all corpora and both languages, indicating that the translations maintain the vocabulary richness of the originals.

4. **Expansion ratios are consistent with known EN-ES patterns.** The mean expansion of 4-9% with low standard deviation confirms that the translations are neither truncated nor inflated at a systematic level.

5. **Spanish consistently has more types than English (17-57% more).** This is the expected behavior for a morphologically rich language and confirms that the translations use proper Spanish morphology rather than word-for-word calques.

### Concerns and Limitations

1. **FEVER source duplicates (11 articles).** These should be documented as a known limitation. They do not affect translation quality but reduce the effective unique content by 0.11%.

2. **Potential truncation in FEVER (31 articles with expansion < 0.85).** These articles may contain incomplete translations. They represent 0.31% of the corpus.

3. **WikiText truncation (3 articles with expansion < 0.90).** Investigation confirmed 3 articles with genuine truncation caused by the Stage 4 consensus merge failing to include all sections. The remaining 88 sub-1.0 articles are explained by legitimate compression from content rich in proper nouns, numbers, and structured data (credits, charts, discographies) that does not expand in translation.

4. **Sentence splitter artifacts.** The maximum sentence lengths (up to 1,616 words) are artifacts of the regex-based sentence splitter, not real sentences. This affects syntactic statistics but not the underlying data quality.

5. **Classification case inconsistency in WikiText.** A minor metadata normalization issue that should be fixed in the pipeline.

6. **FEVER sports bias (66.8%).** While documented and expected from the stratified sampling, users should be aware that the corpus is not topically balanced.

These metrics are computed across all three datasets together to assess the corpus as a unified resource.

| Metric | English | Spanish |

**Key finding: The combined corpus contains 11.5M English tokens and 11.9M Spanish tokens across 12,003 bilingual documents.** This is a substantial bilingual resource — approximately 23.5 million tokens total across both languages.

**Vocabulary overlap is significant but not excessive.** The sum of individual vocabularies (174,876 EN types) is 49% larger than the combined vocabulary (117,297 EN types). This means 57,579 English word types (33% of the sum) appear in more than one dataset. This is a healthy level of overlap — the datasets share a common core of general vocabulary while each contributing substantial exclusive vocabulary. If the overlap were near zero, the datasets would have nothing in common (unlikely for natural language). If the overlap were near 100%, the datasets would be redundant.

Spanish shows a similar pattern with 33% overlap (74,879 shared types out of 225,624 sum). The Spanish combined vocabulary (150,745) is 29% larger than English (117,297), consistent with the morphological richness of Spanish observed in the per-dataset metrics.

### 7.3 Cross-Dataset Duplicate Detection

| Language | Cross-Dataset Duplicate Pairs |
|----------|------------------------------|
| English | 0 |
| Spanish | 0 |

**No cross-dataset duplicates were found.** Despite WikiText and FEVER both sourcing from Wikipedia, the selection process produced completely independent subsets with no shared articles. This is a strong positive finding — it means the 12,003 bilingual entries are all unique across the entire corpus.

This result is notable because it was not guaranteed by design. WikiText contains full Wikipedia articles while FEVER contains shorter passages extracted from Wikipedia. It was plausible that a FEVER passage could be a fragment of a WikiText article. The zero-duplicate result confirms that either the articles are genuinely different, or the passages are too short to match the full articles via exact hash comparison.

**Note:** This detection uses exact text matching (MD5 hash). Near-duplicates (e.g., a FEVER passage that is a subset of a WikiText article) would not be caught by this method. A more sophisticated substring or containment analysis would be needed to detect partial overlaps.

## 8. Recommendations

1. **For the paper**: Report the Zipf exponents, MATTR, MTLD, expansion ratios, duplicate counts, and vocabulary overlap as primary quality indicators. These metrics tell a clear story: the generated translations behave like natural language, maintain lexical diversity, and are neither truncated nor duplicated. The zero cross-dataset duplicates and vocabulary complementarity results are particularly strong findings that validate the corpus design.

2. **For the dataset**: Consider adding a `quality_flag` field to entries with expansion ratio below 0.85, so downstream users can filter them out if needed.

3. **For the pipeline**: Normalize classification strings to lowercase in the metadata extraction stage to prevent case inconsistencies. The current 53 categories should be collapsed to a standardized set of 15-20 through a normalization mapping (e.g., merging "Science"/"science", consolidating "military" under "history").

4. **For the combined corpus**: Report the corpus as 23.5M tokens across 12,003 unique bilingual documents with zero cross-dataset overlap. Highlight the vocabulary complementarity: each dataset contributes exclusive vocabulary (WikiText 39%, FEVER 19%, Europarl 9% of the combined English vocabulary), confirming the value of using three diverse sources.

5. **For downstream users**: Document that the combined corpus is heavily skewed toward sports (56.4%), driven by FEVER. Users needing topical balance should either subsample or weight by category. Europarl is the most topically focused (politics/economy/human rights) while WikiText provides the broadest coverage.

6. **For future work**: Compute inter-model agreement from the pipeline JSON files to validate that the multi-model approach produces genuinely diverse candidates. Additionally, creating a human-translated reference for 30-50 FEVER articles would enable BLEU/chrF/COMET scores. A containment analysis (checking if FEVER passages are substrings of WikiText articles) would complement the exact-match duplicate detection.
